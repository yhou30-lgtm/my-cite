What does it mean to live a good life?

One might answer that it means being happy, enjoying one's experiences, and avoiding suffering. After all, it is difficult to imagine someone living a genuinely miserable life and yet claiming to be doing exceptionally well. But is happiness really all that matters? Suppose you could experience a lifetime of extraordinary happiness, friendship, achievement, and love, only to discover that none of these things had actually happened. Would your life still have been a good one?

This question lies at the heart of one of the simplest and most influential theories of well-being: hedonism.

In Chapter 1 of The Philosophy of Well-Being, the philosopher Fletcher introduces hedonism, examines the arguments in its favour, and considers several objections that challenge its central claim. Among them is Robert Nozick's famous experience machine, a thought experiment that forces us to reconsider whether feeling good is sufficient for living well.

1. What Is Hedonism?

Hedonism, in its simplest form, is the view that all and only pleasure is good for us, and all and only pain is bad for us.

According to this theory, a person's overall well-being is determined entirely by the balance between pleasure and pain they experience. Fletcher calls this balance a person's hedonic level.

It is important to distinguish between two kinds of value here: instrumental and non-instrumental value.

Something has instrumental value when it is valuable because it helps us obtain something else. Money, for instance, is useful because it allows us to purchase things that bring us pleasure or relieve our suffering. Friendship, a comfortable home, and even medicine can similarly be valuable as means to a more pleasant existence.

Non-instrumental value, on the other hand, refers to something that is good in itself, rather than merely because it produces some further benefit.

The hedonist argues that pleasure is the only thing that possesses non-instrumental value for us. Everything else is valuable only insofar as it contributes to pleasure or helps us avoid pain.

The appeal of this position is intuitive. Consider two hypothetical lives. In the first, someone experiences deep happiness, meaningful relationships, professional success, and very little suffering. In the second, someone experiences constant anxiety, disappointment, and physical pain. Most of us would naturally judge the first life to be better.

This seems to suggest that pleasure and pain play a fundamental role in determining well-being.

However, there is an important distinction between saying that pleasure contributes to a good life and saying that pleasure is the only thing that contributes to it. The former is relatively easy to accept; the latter is much more difficult to establish.

2. Arguments in Favour of Hedonism

Fletcher considers four arguments that provide support for hedonism. Although each offers an intuitive reason to take the theory seriously, none conclusively establishes that pleasure and pain are the only determinants of well-being.

2.1 The Argument from Correlation

Consider Raj, who is enjoying a rollercoaster ride. His experience is initially pleasurable, but a bird suddenly flies into his face, causing considerable pain. After the ride, his friend gives him painkillers and buys him some candy floss, which he enjoys.

There are two things happening simultaneously. First, Raj's hedonic level decreases when he experiences pain and increases again when he receives relief and pleasure. Second, his well-being appears to follow exactly the same pattern.

The hedonist argues that this correlation is evidence that well-being is entirely determined by one's hedonic level.

However, Fletcher identifies a problem: correlation does not necessarily establish causation, nor does it demonstrate that one variable is the sole explanation of another.

Even if Raj's pleasure and pain influence his well-being, other factors may contribute independently. Imagine that Raj has the same hedonic level in two different situations, but in one of them he also has stronger friendships, greater self-confidence, more knowledge, and has achieved his most important life goals. It seems plausible that he would be better off in the second situation.

The same problem appears in the argument from paradigm cases: people whose lives seem to be going particularly well generally experience considerable pleasure and relatively little pain. But this does not prove that pleasure alone explains their well-being.

Fletcher illustrates the problem through the correlation between ice cream sales and drownings. Both tend to increase when temperatures rise. However, buying ice cream does not cause people to drown, nor do drownings cause people to buy ice cream. Temperature is the third variable that explains the correlation.

Similarly, perhaps pleasure and well-being correlate because both are influenced by other aspects of a good life.

The argument therefore establishes something relatively modest: pleasure and pain are relevant to well-being. It does not establish that they are all that matter.

2.2 The Argument from Welfare Subjects

Another argument concerns the kinds of things capable of having well-being in the first place.

Consider a pencil, a telephone, and a dog. We would hardly say that a pencil or a telephone has a level of well-being. A dog, however, can clearly experience pleasure and pain, and it seems reasonable to say that its life can go better or worse.

The hedonist points out that there is a striking overlap between the things capable of having well-being and the things capable of experiencing pleasure and pain.

This provides some support for the theory: perhaps the capacity to experience pleasure and pain is what makes something a subject of well-being.

Nevertheless, the argument faces a similar difficulty. Dogs and dolphins are not merely capable of experiencing pleasure and pain; they can also form desires, develop relationships, acquire knowledge, and achieve certain goals. Pencils and telephones cannot do any of these things either.

Consequently, the distinction between welfare subjects and non-welfare subjects may not depend exclusively on the capacity for pleasure and pain.

2.3 The Motivational Argument

A third argument attempts to establish hedonism through human motivation.

It rests on two propositions:

  1. All human actions aim to maximise one's own well-being.
  2. Pleasure and pain are the only things capable of motivating us.

If both propositions are true, the hedonist might conclude that pleasure and pain must be the only things that contribute to well-being.

However, both premises are highly controversial.

Consider someone who donates money to charity anonymously. They receive no recognition, financial reward, or obvious personal advantage. Or consider a soldier who throws himself onto a grenade to save his fellow soldiers, knowing that doing so will almost certainly cost him his life.

It seems implausible to describe these actions as attempts to maximise personal well-being.

Furthermore, people do not always act simply to obtain pleasure or avoid suffering. Sometimes we act because we believe something is morally right, because someone needs our help, or because we want to preserve something important for future generations.

This raises a fundamental question: Are we motivated by the things that make us happy, or can we also be motivated by things that we consider valuable in themselves?

If the latter is possible, then the motivational argument for hedonism becomes considerably weaker.

2.4 The Experience Requirement

The final argument introduces what Fletcher calls the experience requirement:

If something contributes to a person's well-being, it must somehow affect that person's experience.

Pleasure and pain clearly satisfy this requirement because they necessarily influence how we experience the world. The hedonist therefore argues that they provide a natural foundation for understanding well-being.

But there are two difficulties.

First, it is not entirely clear that everything affecting our well-being must affect our experience. Consider the possibility of posthumous harm: could something that happens after a person's death make their life worse for them? If so, that would challenge the experience requirement.

Second, even if we accept the experience requirement, pleasure and pain are not the only things that influence our experiences. Happiness and self-respect, for instance, seem distinct from pleasure while still affecting how we experience our lives.

Thus, the experience requirement might establish that well-being must have some connection to experience, but it does not necessarily establish that well-being is reducible to pleasure and pain.

3. The Experience Machine: Would You Choose an Artificially Perfect Life?

Perhaps the most compelling objection to hedonism comes from the American philosopher Robert Nozick.

In Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), Nozick introduces a thought experiment known as the experience machine.

Imagine a machine capable of producing any experience you desire. Through sophisticated stimulation of your brain, it could make you believe that you are writing a brilliant novel, developing meaningful friendships, travelling around the world, or achieving extraordinary success.

In reality, however, you would be floating in a tank, connected to electrodes. Nothing you experience would actually be happening.

You would never know the difference.

Would you choose to connect yourself to this machine for the rest of your life?

At first glance, the answer might seem straightforward. If the machine can provide unlimited pleasure and eliminate suffering, why would anyone refuse?

Yet many people would hesitate.

Fletcher develops a more controlled version of Nozick's thought experiment through two characters: Trudy and Flora.

Trudy lives an extraordinarily fulfilling life. She conducts groundbreaking scientific research, runs marathons, works for charity, maintains close friendships, raises a family, and writes critically acclaimed novels.

Flora, by contrast, has been connected to an experience machine since birth. She experiences all the same achievements, relationships, and pleasures as Trudy. To her, everything feels completely real.

Fletcher stipulates that Trudy and Flora have identical hedonic levels at every moment of their lives.

According to hedonism, the two women must therefore have exactly the same level of well-being.

But is that really plausible?

Suppose you were given the choice of which life your child should live. Would you choose Trudy's actual life or Flora's artificial one?

Most people would probably choose Trudy.

The reason seems to be that we value more than simply experiencing happiness. We want our friendships to be genuine, our achievements to be real, and our knowledge to reflect the actual world.

As Nozick famously suggests, we want to do certain things, not merely have the experience of doing them. We also want to be a certain kind of person.

This is a powerful challenge to hedonism. If Trudy and Flora have identical hedonic levels but Trudy is nevertheless better off, then pleasure and pain cannot be the only determinants of well-being.

3.1 How Can a Hedonist Respond?

The experience machine is not necessarily the end of the debate. Fletcher considers four possible responses that a hedonist might offer.

1. Status quo bias

Perhaps we reject Flora's life simply because we are accustomed to living in the real world. We naturally favour the situation we believe ourselves to occupy and are reluctant to abandon it.

The hedonist might argue that our preference for Trudy's life reflects this psychological bias rather than a genuine difference in well-being.

2. Symmetry between pleasure and pain

Consider the opposite situation. Suppose Flora experiences pain inside the machine. Would that pain be less bad for her simply because it is artificial?

Most people would probably say no. An artificially produced painful experience seems just as unpleasant as a real one, other things being equal.

The hedonist can therefore question why we should treat artificial pleasure differently from artificial pain.

However, this response does not fully address the objection. Even if artificial pleasure and pain have exactly the same value as their real counterparts, we might still believe that genuine achievement, friendship, and knowledge contribute independently to well-being.

3. Hidden pain

Perhaps our judgement that Flora's life is inferior comes from imagining that she will eventually discover the deception. Upon discovering that her entire life has been artificial, she might experience profound disappointment and suffering.

However, this response is problematic because Fletcher explicitly stipulates that Trudy and Flora have identical hedonic levels throughout their lives. Introducing Flora's future suffering would undermine the conditions of the thought experiment.

4. Evolutionary debunking

This is perhaps the most philosophically interesting response.

The hedonist might argue that our belief in the value of friendship, achievement, and genuine experiences is merely a product of evolution.

Throughout human evolutionary history, individuals who valued social relationships and personal achievements may have been more likely to survive and reproduce. Consequently, evolution could have encouraged us to believe that these things are valuable, regardless of whether they actually contribute to well-being.

In other words, perhaps our intuition that Trudy's life is better than Flora's is simply an evolutionary by-product rather than a reliable judgement about what makes a life good.

However, Fletcher identifies an important weakness in this argument.

The same evolutionary explanation can be applied to pleasure and pain. Eating, reproduction, and recreation are generally pleasurable and have historically contributed to survival. Conversely, injury and starvation are painful and threaten survival.

If evolution can explain why we believe friendship and achievement are valuable without establishing that they genuinely are, it can also explain why we believe pleasure is valuable.

The hedonist therefore risks undermining the very intuition upon which their own theory depends.

Ultimately, the experience machine suggests that there is a difference between feeling that one's life is going well and one's life actually going well.

4. The Problem of Base Pleasures

The experience machine challenges the idea that only pleasure contributes to well-being. The second major objection, known as the base pleasures objection, challenges a different part of hedonism: the claim that all pleasure is good for us.

Consider three kinds of pleasure:

  • Immoral pleasures, such as enjoying the suffering of another person.
  • Trivial pleasures, such as spending hours watching reality television.
  • Undignified pleasures, such as taking pleasure in wallowing in mud like a pig.

The objection is relatively intuitive. Surely not every pleasurable experience makes someone's life better?

For instance, imagine someone who derives enormous satisfaction from torturing others. Even if this person experiences genuine pleasure, we might hesitate to say that this pleasure contributes positively to their well-being.

The challenge is that hedonism appears committed to counting every pleasure as beneficial, regardless of its source.

Fletcher considers two possible responses.

4.1 Instrumental Disvalue Distractions

The first response is that we may be confusing the immediate pleasure with its likely consequences.

A person who enjoys torturing others might develop a sadistic personality, destroy their relationships, and eventually face punishment. These consequences would undoubtedly make their life worse.

However, the objection is that even if we remove all these negative consequences, we may still feel that the pleasure derived from torture has no genuine value for the person experiencing it.

In other words, perhaps the problem is not what the pleasure leads to, but the nature of the pleasure itself.

4.2 Confusing Moral Value with Prudential Value

The second response is more subtle.

The hedonist might argue that we are confusing two different questions:

  • Is something morally good?
  • Is something good for the person experiencing it?

These are not necessarily the same.

An action can be morally reprehensible while still producing pleasure for the individual performing it. The hedonist argues that we should not automatically conclude that this pleasure has no value for that individual simply because its source is immoral.

Fletcher introduces an interesting comparison involving three torturers:

  • Torturer 1: enjoys torturing. Hedonic level +10.
  • Torturer 2: feels nothing. Hedonic level 0.
  • Torturer 3: suffers while torturing. Hedonic level -10.

If you had to choose which of these three individuals to be, who would you choose?

A strict hedonist would presumably argue that Torturer 1 is best off, since their hedonic level is the highest.

Some people might agree. After all, from the perspective of immediate personal pleasure, enjoying an activity seems better than being indifferent to it or suffering through it.

Others, however, might still prefer to be Torturer 2 or even Torturer 3. This suggests that the objection cannot be dismissed simply by distinguishing moral value from personal well-being.

Perhaps there really is something troubling about taking pleasure in certain activities, even when that pleasure produces no additional negative consequences.

5. Are All Pleasures Equally Valuable?

There is another, slightly different version of the base pleasures objection.

This time, the argument does not claim that certain pleasures have no value. Instead, it challenges the assumption that all pleasures of equal intensity and duration must have equal value.

Consider two activities:

  • Spending three hours wallowing in mud like a pig.
  • Spending three hours reading Shakespeare's Hamlet.

Suppose both activities generate exactly the same hedonic level, represented by +10.

Does this mean that they contribute equally to the person's well-being?

Many people would instinctively say no. Reading Hamlet seems to offer something more valuable than the purely sensory pleasure of wallowing in mud.

The difficulty for traditional quantitative hedonism is that it evaluates pleasure according to its quantity, particularly its intensity and duration. If two experiences generate equal amounts of pleasure, they should contribute equally to well-being.

But perhaps the quality of pleasure matters too.

5.1 John Stuart Mill and Qualitative Hedonism

This brings us to the nineteenth-century philosopher John Stuart Mill, who developed a more sophisticated version of hedonism.

Mill argued that pleasures differ not only in quantity but also in quality.

As he famously writes in Utilitarianism (1863):

"It would be absurd to think that while, in estimating all other things, quality is considered as well as quantity, the estimation of pleasures should be supposed to depend on quantity alone."

According to qualitative hedonism, intellectual pleasures, such as reading literature or engaging in philosophical reflection, may possess a higher quality than purely physical or sensory pleasures.

This allows a hedonist to argue that reading Hamlet can contribute more to well-being than wallowing in mud, even if both experiences have the same intensity and duration.

However, this position introduces a new philosophical difficulty.

If only pleasure has intrinsic value, how can one pleasure be intrinsically better than another?

A common criticism is that the difference in value must ultimately come from the activities producing the pleasures. Reading Shakespeare is considered more valuable because literature itself is more valuable than wallowing in mud.

But if the activities themselves have independent value, then pleasure is no longer the only thing that matters.

Fletcher offers a possible response: perhaps pleasures themselves have different intrinsic natures.

In other words, the pleasure of reading Hamlet might not simply be a larger quantity of the same mental experience produced by wallowing in mud. The two experiences might be qualitatively different in their own right.

If this is true, qualitative hedonism remains a possible position. It can recognise different kinds of pleasure without necessarily assigning independent value to the activities that produce them.

The debate ultimately raises a deeper question about the nature of pleasure itself: are all pleasures fundamentally the same kind of mental state, differing only in intensity and duration, or are there fundamentally different kinds of pleasure?

6. So, Is Pleasure All There Is to a Good Life?

After examining the arguments and objections, where does Fletcher's discussion leave us?

Importantly, none of the objections establishes that pleasure and pain are irrelevant to well-being. The experience machine does not demonstrate that Flora's pleasures have no value. The base pleasures objection does not necessarily establish that every morally questionable pleasure is worthless.

Rather, these objections challenge the much stronger proposition that pleasure and pain are the only things that determine how well a person's life goes.

This distinction is crucial.

Hedonism offers an attractive and relatively straightforward account of well-being. Pleasure matters, suffering matters, and it is difficult to imagine a genuinely good life completely detached from either.

Nevertheless, the experience machine suggests that genuine achievements, meaningful relationships, and knowledge may possess value beyond the pleasure they generate. Meanwhile, the base pleasures objection raises doubts about whether every pleasurable experience should count equally towards a good life.

My main takeaway from Fletcher's chapter is that a good life may require more than simply feeling good about it.

Imagine two people with exactly the same level of happiness. One has developed meaningful friendships, pursued ambitious goals, acquired knowledge, and made a genuine contribution to the world. The other has experienced an equally pleasurable life, but none of these things has actually happened.

If we believe the first person is better off, then we are already accepting that something beyond pleasure contributes to well-being.

Perhaps the purpose of a good life is not merely to maximise the pleasure we experience, but also to pursue things that are worth experiencing in the first place.

7. A Question Worth Thinking About

Fletcher concludes the chapter with a question that I find particularly interesting:

What do the pleasure of reading a great novel and the pleasure of going on a waterslide have in common?

Both are pleasurable experiences, but they seem to differ in their nature. One may involve intellectual engagement, imagination, and reflection, while the other offers immediate physical excitement.

If pleasure is the only thing that makes these experiences valuable, what exactly is the common feature that gives them both their value?

And if they are fundamentally different kinds of pleasure, can we really measure them on the same scale?

Perhaps the most interesting question is not whether pleasure matters to a good life. It clearly does. The harder question is whether pleasure alone can explain why some lives seem more worth living than others.
